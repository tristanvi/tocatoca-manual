# TocaToca Concepts

This document explains the ideas behind TocaToca in plain language. It is written for music lovers, DJs, collectors, dancers, and tango enthusiasts who want to understand how to explore the catalogue.

Use these explanations as source material for future manual pages, FAQ answers, captions, and onboarding text.

## Historical Shellac Recordings

### What it is

Shellac recordings are historical records from the early recording era, before modern vinyl records and digital releases. Many tango recordings from the 1920s, 1930s, and 1940s were first published on shellac discs.

### Why it exists

This is the musical world TocaToca focuses on: orchestras, singers, composers, lyricists, labels, and recordings from the shellac era.

### How users benefit

Users can listen to historical recordings without needing the original discs, specialist equipment, or private archives. DJs and collectors can compare versions, discover related artists, and build listening paths through the music.

## TangoTunes Transfers

### What it is

TangoTunes transfers are carefully prepared digital versions of historical tango recordings. They preserve the character of the original recordings while making them easier to hear today.

### Why it exists

Historical records are fragile, rare, and often difficult to play well. Transfers make the music accessible while respecting the source.

### How users benefit

Listeners can stream high-quality versions of classic recordings directly in TocaToca. DJs can prepare tandas, collectors can compare takes and orchestras, and dancers can explore familiar music in greater detail.

## Collections

### What it is

A collection is a group of recordings that belong together in TocaToca. In the current app interface, this area may still appear as `Albums`, but the manual uses `collections` in English and `Sammlungen` in German.

### Why it exists

Historical catalogues do not always fit modern album language. A collection may represent a release, a curated group, a transfer set, or another meaningful grouping of recordings.

### How users benefit

Collections give users a clear entry point. Instead of starting with a long list of individual recordings, users can open a group, listen through it, and then follow artist or song links from there.

## Recordings

### What it is

A recording is one specific recorded performance. In the interface, it may appear as a song or title.

### Why it exists

Tango history is full of different versions, dates, orchestras, singers, and arrangements. Treating each recording as its own item helps preserve those differences.

### How users benefit

Users can compare recordings of the same tango, find a preferred orchestra or singer, and understand how a piece changed across time and performers.

## Orchestra Relationships

### What it is

Many tango recordings are strongly connected to an orchestra. The orchestra is often the main identity of the recording, especially for dancers and DJs.

### Why it exists

Orchestras shaped the sound, rhythm, phrasing, and style of tango recordings. A tango by Di Sarli, D'Arienzo, Troilo, or Pugliese can feel very different even when the composition is the same.

### How users benefit

Users can follow orchestra names to discover related recordings. DJs can build sets around a sound. Collectors can compare periods, singers, and labels connected to the same orchestra.

## Singer Relationships

### What it is

Many recordings include a singer, and the singer may be just as important to the listening experience as the orchestra.

### Why it exists

Singers bring phrasing, emotion, and identity to tango recordings. The same orchestra can sound different with different voices.

### How users benefit

Users can follow singer names to find more recordings with that voice. This is useful for dancers who love a particular singer, DJs building a vocal tanda, and collectors studying collaborations.

## Why One Tango May Exist in Many Recordings

### What it is

One tango title can appear many times in TocaToca because different orchestras, singers, years, labels, or takes may exist.

### Why it exists

Tango is a living repertoire. Popular pieces were recorded repeatedly, sometimes across decades. A title is not the same thing as a recording.

### How users benefit

Users can compare interpretations instead of seeing only one result. A DJ might prefer one orchestra for dancing. A collector might want every known version. A listener might discover that a familiar tango has a completely different character in another recording.

## Artist-Link Behaviour

### What it is

When a user clicks an artist name in TocaToca, the app opens a useful song list with available recordings connected to that artist.

### Why it exists

TocaToca is designed for discovery. A direct song list is often more helpful than a static artist profile, because users can immediately listen.

### How users benefit

Users can move naturally through the catalogue:

- from a collection to an orchestra
- from an orchestra to its recordings
- from a song to a singer
- from a singer to other collaborations
- from a familiar name to unfamiliar music

This makes artist links one of the best ways to explore TocaToca.

## Streaming-Only Access

### What it is

TocaToca is for streaming. Users listen in the browser instead of downloading audio files.

### Why it exists

Streaming keeps access simple. Users can open the player, sign in, and listen without managing files.

### How users benefit

The music is available wherever the user can open TocaToca with an active account. DJs and listeners can browse, compare, favorite, and build playlists without maintaining a separate file library.

## Search Strategies

### What it is

Search in TocaToca helps users find recordings, artists, and collections. Good search often means trying more than one path.

### Why it exists

Historical music data can include variant spellings, different roles, multiple artists, and catalogue-specific naming. A single exact search may miss something useful.

### How users benefit

Users get better results when they search flexibly:

- Try a full title first.
- If there are too few results, try one distinctive word.
- Search by orchestra name.
- Search by singer name.
- Search by composer or lyricist when available.
- Open a collection and follow names from there.
- Click artist links to see connected recordings.

This approach is especially helpful for tango titles with many recordings or historical names with variant spellings.

## Discovering Music Through Artists

### What it is

Artist discovery means using names as paths through the catalogue. An artist can be an orchestra, singer, composer, lyricist, arranger, or another contributor.

### Why it exists

Tango recordings are built from relationships. A title, an orchestra, a singer, a composer, and a period can all matter. Artist-based discovery reflects how tango lovers often think and listen.

### How users benefit

Music lovers can follow curiosity. DJs can find related recordings for programming. Collectors can study collaborations. Dancers can move from a favorite orchestra or singer into a wider musical world.

Artist discovery turns TocaToca from a simple player into a listening map.
